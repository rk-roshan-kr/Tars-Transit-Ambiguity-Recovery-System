\section{Results} \label{sec:results}

This section presents the empirical validation of the TARS v1 framework. All results reported in this section are evaluated exclusively on our independent, manually labeled Blind Validation Partition (see Table~\ref{tab:dataset_overview} and Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_workflow} in Section~\ref{sec:method_dataset_overview}) consisting of $N = \BlindLCCount$ TESS light curves across $N_{\text{stars}} = \BlindStarCount$ unique systems.

\subsection{Validation Campaign Hierarchy} \label{sec:results_hierarchy}
To ensure the scientific defensibility of our findings, we execute a structured validation hierarchy:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Level 1: Overall Performance}: Compare AUROC, AUPRC, and ECE across 4 model configurations to establish baseline performance.
\item \textbf{Level 2: Bootstrap Stability}: Use 1,000 resamples to estimate confidence intervals and variances.
\item \textbf{Level 3: Component Ablation}: Measure ECE and AUROC changes when features are omitted.
\item \textbf{Level 4: Calibration Audit}: Examine reliability curves and OLS slope attenuation biases.
\item \textbf{Level 5: Sensitivity Sweeps}: Evaluate Conditional Mutual Information across bin counts.
\item \textbf{Level 6: Noise Perturbations}: Stress test using synthetic noise, drop masks, and biases.
\item \textbf{Level 7: Cohort Failure Analysis}: Contrast dwarfs vs. giant star network topologies to find physical limits.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bootstrap Stability Analysis} \label{sec:results_bootstrap}
We perform bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations on the Blind Validation Partition to estimate distribution overlaps. The mean difference in AUROC between the RAI-only model and the Linear Stack is $\Delta\text{AUROC} = 0.0378$, with a 95\% confidence interval of $[-0.0089, 0.1386]$.

The 95\% confidence intervals of all models overlap significantly (Table~\ref{tab:model_performance}). Because the interval of the difference encompasses zero, the performance differences are statistically indistinguishable under bootstrap resampling. Within the measured uncertainty, the single-feature RAI model achieves comparable ranking performance to complex multi-feature stacks. This suggests that complex classifiers are not extracting unique predictive information beyond what is captured by the Recovery Ambiguity Index.

The reported raw mean and median SNR values are extraordinarily large (of order $10^{15}$ to $10^{16}$). This is due to a units mismatch in the validation script's raw SNR calculation: transit depth is loaded in parts-per-million (ppm; mean $\mu_{\text{depth}} = 4,058$), while the residual scatter (\texttt{residual\_mad}) is loaded in fractional flux (mean $\mu_{\text{mad}} = 0.000114$). Dividing ppm by fractional flux scales the raw ratio by a factor of $10^6$. When combined with the baseline time-squared scaling multiplier, this mismatch inflates the raw values. This SNR scale anomaly is a software reporting artifact and not a scientific result.

Because TARS standardizes all input features (Z-scoring) before computing the RAI, the classification model was completely insulated from this raw scale shift. If depth is converted to fractional flux, the resulting SNRs scale down by exactly $10^6$, returning realistic SNRs in the range of 10 to 100. This highlights the value of unsupervised Z-score normalization in preventing scale-induced classification failures.

The RAI is **not applicable** to evolved stellar hosts ($\log g < 4.0$). Evolved stars exhibit low-frequency convective granulation noise and photospheric oscillations. The Stage 3 period recoverer misinterprets these physical stellar oscillations as periodic transits, leading to severe candidate event inflation ($46.07$ candidates vs. $37.92$ for dwarfs) and a denser, more complex alias network topology. This deforms the graph structure, de-calibrates the Z-score features, and breaks the ambiguity assumptions of the RAI.

Empirical validation on our independent Blind Validation Partition confirms this performance collapse: for the $N=6$ giant-hosted targets, the RAI achieves an AUROC of $0.2000$ and a high calibration error (ECE = $0.4086$; see Table~\ref{tab:distribution_shift}), which is no better than random guessing. This cohort represents approximately $4\%$ of the targets observed in wide-field transit surveys like TESS, primarily concentrated in asteroseismology programs.

\textbf{User Guidance}: Users should restrict the application of the RAI to dwarf stellar hosts ($\log g \ge 4.0$) or implement separate calibration baselines specifically for evolved hosts. Future work will focus on adapting the Stage 3 recovery algorithm to distinguish convective granulation scales from true planetary transits on giant hosts.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency} \label{sec:results_efficiency}
The calculation of the five RAI sub-features from the candidate period set scales as $O(N_{\text{final}}^2)$ due to the pairwise comparison of periods during alias graph construction. On a single standard CPU core, the features are computed in $< 0.1$ seconds. This execution speed represents a significant improvement over the minutes to hours required by FPP validation tools. For example, Vespa \citep{Morton2012, Morton2015} requires typically 5 to 30 minutes per candidate to run MCMC stellar population simulations of blend scenarios. TARS v1, by focusing on epistemic recovery ambiguity, achieves a speed-up of several orders of magnitude, making it suitable for real-time pre-filtering of massive catalogs.

\subsubsection{PCA Loading Stability and Design Rationale}
To test the design rationale of using fixed physical sign weights ($+1, +1, +1, -1, -1$) rather than empirical weights, we fit a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) model on the five standardised features. The comparison is detailed in Table~\ref{tab:pca_comparison}. 

The first principal component (PC1) explain $64.79\%$ of the variance. Its loading weights match the sign of our fixed weights. Under $100$ bootstrap resamples of the training set, the PCA loadings exhibit a sign-inversion rate of $6.0\%$, demonstrating that data-driven weights are vulnerable to sign flips under minor training set variations. Furthermore, the PC1 model achieves comparable blind performance (AUROC = $0.6668$, ECE = $0.0456$) to the fixed RAI (AUROC = $0.6732$, ECE = $0.0644$), validating that the fixed-weight physical construct is statistically robust and prevents overfitting.

\subsubsection{Multivariable Logistic Regression of Vetting Limits}
To evaluate the sensitivity limits and control for confounding variables, we fitted a multivariable logistic regression mapping strict recovery to recovered Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), injected period ($P_{\text{inj}}$), and injected transit depth in parts-per-thousand ($D_{\text{inj, ppt}} = D_{\text{inj}} \times 1000$ to prevent numerical convergence issues):
\begin{equation}
\ln\left(\frac{p_{\text{rec}}}{1 - p_{\text{rec}}}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{SNR} + \beta_2 P_{\text{inj}} + \beta_3 D_{\text{inj, ppt}}

The fitted model coefficients and their $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals (obtained via $1,000$ resamples) are:
\begin{itemize}

\end{itemize}

To quantify the collinearity between predictors, we compute the Variance Inflation Factors (VIF):
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Transit Depth VIF}: $1.658$

Although transit depth and SNR are physically related, the measured VIF values ($\le 1.675$) indicate that predictor collinearity is low. The preregistration for EXP-005 (Appendix, \texttt{docs/EXP-005\_preregistration.md}) described TARS as a two-stage system in which Astropy BLS provides the initial threshold detection and the TARS vetting layer (RAI scoring) performs ambiguity resolution on BLS candidates. \textbf{Preregistered question:} Does TARS vetting (applied to BLS candidates) improve F1-score and ROC-AUC relative to unvetted BLS and TLS?

\textbf{Executed question:} What is the operating envelope of an event-space architecture (TARS Stages 2--3 standalone) relative to classical phase-folding methods (BLS, TLS) across the full range of injected transit depths?

EXP-005 compares the standalone TARS configuration with classical phase-folding detectors. Because the preregistered deployment architecture placed TARS downstream of threshold detection, these results primarily characterise the operating envelope of the standalone configuration rather than the intended hybrid pipeline.

\subsubsection{Operating Envelope of the Event-Space Architecture} \label{sec:exp5_physical_explanation}

The methodology (Section~\ref{sec:method_pipeline_flow}) defines Stage~2 as operating on individually distinguishable brightness dips, with the documented failure mode: \textit{``Signals with highly eccentric orbits or single-transit events are missed.''}  This is a special case of a more general constraint: Stage~2 requires each individual transit to exceed the local noise floor ($S_i \ge 3\sigma$) before it enters the Stage~3 hypothesis space. No folding or coherent averaging across multiple transits is performed.

The injected signals in EXP-005 span 100--9720~ppm against a median local noise of $\sigma_\text{local} \approx 450$--1800~ppm per cadence. For the $N \approx 5$--11 transits visible within a single TESS sector, a phase-folding algorithm accumulates an effective SNR boost of $\sqrt{N} \approx 2$--3, bringing many sub-threshold injections above detectability. TARS does not benefit from this boost. The consequence is a well-defined single-event SNR floor: only injections with per-transit depth $\gtrsim 2356$~ppm were individually visible above $3\sigma$ in the available sector, and only those 12 runs were recovered.

This is the \textbf{operating boundary} of the event-space architecture, not a deficiency relative to BLS or TLS. The two architectures are answering different questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase-folding (BLS, TLS)}: ``Does a periodic signal exist, even if no single transit is individually visible?'' Recovery is sensitivity-limited by $\sqrt{N_\text{transits}}$ coherent averaging.
    \item \textbf{Event-space (TARS)}: ``Are individually visible events consistent with a single orbit?'' Recovery requires individual events to already exceed the noise floor; it then achieves period precision of $<0.05\%$ at 2,879--9,603$\times$ lower computational cost per candidate.
\end{itemize}

In a complete exoplanet vetting pipeline, these architectures are complementary. Phase-folding methods act as threshold detectors over the full depth range; the event-space architecture acts as a fast, explainable confirmation and ambiguity resolution engine for candidates already above the single-event SNR floor. The 100\% TARS false positive rate observed in the FP experiment (Table~\ref{tab:exp5_comparison}) is consistent with this interpretation: in the absence of a threshold detection gate, TARS assigns high probability to any light curve containing individually visible dip events, regardless of periodicity.

\subsection{Cross-Sector RAI Stability (EXP-007)} \label{sec:results_exp007}

\subsubsection{Motivation and Design}

The Recovery Ambiguity Index (RAI) is computed per-sector from Stage~3 alias graph properties. If RAI is a physically meaningful stellar property rather than a noise or window-function artefact, the same target observed in two independent, widely separated TESS sectors should yield consistent RAI values. EXP-007 tests this cross-sector reproducibility by analyzing all targets in the TARS-250K-R1 corpus (Sectors~1--14) that appear in at least two sectors, comparing quiet stars (residual MAD~$<$~500~ppm) and active stars (MAD~$\geq$~500~ppm). All hypotheses and thresholds were preregistered before execution (Preregistration TARS-EXP-007-v1.0, locked 2026-07-27).

\subsubsection{Data and Ingestion Analysis}

Of the 74,674 unique TIC~IDs in \texttt{catalog\_records} with at least one non-null RAI value, 17,377 appear in $\geq$2 sectors. However, MAD values (from the Stage~6 ML training/validation splits) are available for only 959 unique TIC~IDs. After intersecting these sets, we obtain 311 multi-sector targets with MAD data, yielding \textbf{3,517 total sector pairs}: 3,455 from quiet targets ($N_{\text{quiet}}=305$) and 62 from active targets ($N_{\text{active}}=6$).

\paragraph{Stellar Catalog Reduction Analysis.} 
The severe reduction from 74,674 RAI-vetted targets to 959 targets with MAD data is not due to processing failures or database joins. Rather, it reflects the intentional design of the primary TARS ML pipeline: while the database (\texttt{catalog\_records}) stores simple Stage~3 period-vetting outputs for the entire unlabeled 250K corpus, full Stage~1--2 raw feature vectors (including \texttt{residual\_mad}) were only generated, consolidated, and cached on disk for the hand-labeled validation and training subsets ($N=965$ unique stars). This subset-only feature storage represents a major data limitation for EXP-007. Because of this, the active star class is restricted to $N_{\text{active}}=6$ targets, far below the preregistered minimum of $N \geq 100$.

\subsubsection{Hypothesis Outcomes}

\paragraph{H1 (Primary) --- Wilcoxon Rank-Sum.}
Wilcoxon rank-sum test on per-target mean $|\Delta\text{RAI}|$: $U = 703.0$, $p = 0.331$, rank-biserial $r = 0.232$. Because the active star sample size is extremely small ($N_{\text{active}}=6$), the test lacks sufficient statistical power. Thus, H1 is \textbf{inconclusive due to insufficient statistical power} rather than rejected. The direction of the difference is consistent with the hypothesis: quiet stars have a median $|\Delta\text{RAI}|$ of $1.587$ (95\% bootstrap CI: $[1.476,\ 1.746]$) compared to $3.018$ for active stars (95\% CI: $[0.474,\ 4.317]$).

\paragraph{H2a --- Spearman Correlation.}
Spearman $\rho = 0.457$ (95\% bootstrap CI: $[0.430,\ 0.485]$) for quiet star sector pairs. H2a is \textbf{rejected}: the correlation does not meet the preregistered threshold of $\rho > 0.50$ (the 95\% CI upper bound is below 0.50).

\paragraph{H2b --- Stability Fraction (Highest Priority Results).}
Only 216 out of 3,455 quiet-star pairs (6.25\%) satisfy $|\Delta\text{RAI}| < 0.2$ (Wilson 95\% CI: $[5.49\%,\ 7.10\%]$). H2b is \textbf{definitively rejected}: the preregistered threshold of $\geq 60\%$ is not met by a substantial margin. Less than 1 in 16 quiet-star sector pairs exhibits RAI stability at the 0.2 level.

\paragraph{H2c --- OLS Regression.}
OLS regression of $|\Delta\text{RAI}|$ on $\log(1 + \text{MAD}_{\text{ppm}})$ yields a coefficient of $0.241$ ($\text{SE} = 0.036$, $t = 6.77$, $p < 0.0001$, 95\% CI: $[0.171,\ 0.311]$). The intercept is $2.360$ ($\text{SE} = 0.037$, $t = 63.09$, $p < 0.0001$, 95\% CI: $[2.287,\ 2.434]$). H2c is \textbf{confirmed} at the Bonferroni-corrected $\alpha = 0.0125$: stellar activity amplitude is a statistically significant positive predictor of sector-to-sector RAI instability.

\paragraph{H2d --- Gap Fraction.}
Untestable. Per-sector cadence counts are not stored in \texttt{catalog\_records} and require raw FITS re-processing to compute. Documented as a preregistered limitation.

\subsubsection{Scientific Interpretation and Manuscript Implications}

These results challenge the core assumption that the RAI is a stable, intrinsic stellar property. Instead, these results suggest that, within the tested operating envelope, RAI is influenced more strongly by observation-specific factors than by intrinsic stellar activity. Because the RAI is derived from the alias graph, its topology is highly dependent on the specific observational window function (pointing, data gaps, cadence coverage). The same star observed in two sectors will produce different alias structures, and therefore different RAI values.

Stellar activity does play a role in this instability: the confirmed OLS slope of $0.241$ ($p < 0.0001$) shows that active stars experience greater sector-to-sector variation. However, the regression model's \textbf{Adjusted R-squared is only $0.0126$}. Stellar activity explains approximately 1.3\% of the observed variance in cross-sector RAI instability. The remaining variance is unexplained by the present model and is likely influenced by additional observational and algorithmic factors that require further investigation.

Consequently, all downstream analyses interpreting the RAI as a precise single-sector metric must be qualified. Single-sector RAI measurements carry a sector-dependent uncertainty of $\sigma_{|\Delta\text{RAI}|} \approx 2.2$ (Residual Standard Error = 2.193). For the discovery campaign (EXP-018), this means that candidate selection thresholds must be interpreted with this uncertainty boundary in mind.

We update the manuscript conclusion: \textbf{RAI exhibits substantial sector-to-sector variability, and this variability is only partially explained by stellar activity.}

\subsection{Feature Ablation and Component Sensitivity (EXP-009)} \label{sec:results_exp009}

\subsubsection{Motivation and Design}
The TARS RAI metric is computed as a weighted combination of five distinct alias-graph topological features: recovery stability (\texttt{FC\_stability}), graph entropy (\texttt{graph\_entropy}), harmonic density (\texttt{harmonic\_density}), period uniqueness (\texttt{period\_uniqueness}), and candidate concentration (\texttt{candidate\_concentration}). To determine whether all five features contribute independent, non-redundant discriminative information, we performed a systematic leave-one-out feature ablation study and permutation importance analysis.

We trained six logistic regression model configurations (the full 5-feature model and five ablated 4-feature models) on the 80\% training split of the TESS corpus ($N=1253$ stars) and evaluated performance on:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dataset 1}: The 20\% validation split ($N=175$ unique stars) from the real TESS corpus.
    \item \textbf{Dataset 2}: The 2,000 controlled synthetic exoplanet injections from the EXP-004 sensitivity campaign.
\end{enumerate}
Evaluating on both real astrophysical data and controlled injections allows us to test the generalizability and portability of feature importance rankings. On the injection dataset, model probabilities are inverted ($1.0 - P$) to represent recovery likelihood, as the training set associates lower graph complexity with genuine transits.

\subsubsection{Two-Feature Search and Dataset Mismatch}
Evaluating all $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ two-feature combinations confirms the existence of high redundancy. The best two-feature model—using only \texttt{period\_uniqueness} and \texttt{candidate\_concentration}—achieves a validation AUROC of $0.6707$, within $0.0025$ of the full five-feature model. The Spearman rank correlation of feature importance ($\Delta$ AUROC) between the real TESS and injection datasets is $\rho = 0.3000$ ($p = 0.6238$), and the Kendall rank correlation is $\tau = 0.2000$ ($p = 0.8167$). While positive, the small number of features ($N=5$) prevents these rank correlation metrics from achieving formal statistical significance. This weak ranking agreement reflects a clear domain shift: feature importance is not perfectly transferable between synthetic injections and real TESS observations.

\subsubsection{Scientific Interpretation and the Candidate Concentration Anomaly}
Within the evaluated classifier, predictive performance depended most strongly on \texttt{period\_uniqueness} and \texttt{harmonic\_density}. \texttt{graph\_entropy} did not provide measurable incremental predictive value in this model. The best two-feature model achieves performance within 0.0025 AUROC of the full model, demonstrating that the five-component RAI incorporates a high degree of mutual feature redundancy.

Removal of \texttt{candidate\_concentration} substantially improved injection-set performance (+0.1759 AUROC on Dataset 2). This unexpected result indicates that the feature interacts differently across datasets and warrants further investigation. One hypothesis is that this anomaly arises from a feature-sign conflict under domain shift: in the composite RAI model, \texttt{candidate\_concentration} is subtracted, and since the overall RAI coefficient is negative, it enters the log-odds with a net positive sign, opposing the complexity features on recovered injections and compressing class separation. Removing the feature resolves this conflict. However, we treat this interpretation strictly as a hypothesis, and a dedicated audit of feature alignment across domains will be conducted in a subsequent experiment.

\subsection{EXP-023: Robustness Under Realistic Observational Noise}
\label{sec:exp023}

We evaluated the stability of TARS topological ambiguity metrics under five families of
observational perturbation applied to the $N=1891$ benchmark targets from EXP-023
(a total of 39,711 pipeline runs). The pipeline was deterministic, restartable,
and fully automated with no human interaction after launch.

\textbf{Metric Rank Stability and Effect Sizes (H1).} Under moderate Gaussian flux noise
($\sigma = 1\%$), \texttt{Graph\_Entropy} maintained Spearman $\rho = 0.875$
with a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of $0.317$ and a Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) of $0.305$.
Under missing cadence perturbations, \texttt{Graph\_Entropy} maintained high rank correlation
up to $30\%$ missing data (Spearman $\rho = 0.801$, $\text{ICC} = 0.771$). Under flux scaling,
topological features produced $\rho = 1.000$ identically, demonstrating scale invariance of the graph representation.
Figure~\ref{fig:exp023_rho} shows the degradation curves for all five topological features.

\textbf{Decision Stability (H2a).} Conditional on successful Stage 3 candidate recovery
($\ge 2$ detected transit-like events), the frozen Model C (from EXP-011, AUROC = 0.6333)
maintained $100\%$ decision agreement across surviving candidates. We emphasize that decision agreement
is evaluated conditionally on Stage 3 survival; as noise increases, total candidate yield decreases.

\textbf{Synthesis with EXP-012 Detector Bottleneck.} Synthesizing EXP-012 (which identified
event recovery as the primary benchmark bottleneck) with EXP-023 demonstrates that within the
detector's operational regime, TARS ambiguity metrics remain highly stable. The primary limitation
of the current implementation lies in single-sector event detection under low SNR rather than in
ambiguity characterization.
